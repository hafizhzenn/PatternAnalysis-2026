# Failure autopsy

**Pattern:** Across both models, the worst-performing slices share one characteristic: they are all cases of under-segmentation, where prostate tissue is missed rather than wrongly added. Neither model labelled any part of the bladder or rectum as prostate. What differs between the two models is where the failures occur. The 2D U-Net struggles at the base of the prostate, next to the bladder, whereas the 3D Improved U-Net struggles at the apex, near the rectum. In both regions the prostate is at its smallest, which makes it hardest to detect.

**Case 1 - 2D, scan 040_w0 slice 16 (Dice 0.00).** This slice passes through the bladder, and the ground truth contains only a small cluster of prostate pixels along the bladder’s edge, which corresponds to the very top of the gland. The 2D model classified these pixels as bladder or surrounding tissue. This is understandable given how the model works: within a single slice, a handful of prostate pixels touching the bladder is visually very similar to the bladder wall. Because the model processes each slice independently, it cannot use the slices below, where the prostate is much more clearly defined.

**Case 2 - 2D, scans 040_w1/w2/w3, slices 13-20 (Dice 0.00).** The same error appears in all four of the worst 2D slices. Each one is taken at the level of the bladder, contains only a small fragment of prostate, and is missed entirely. The consistency of this pattern suggests a systematic limitation of slice-wise segmentation rather than an isolated mistake. It is also precisely the type of error that additional 3D context is expected to resolve.

**Case 3 - 3D, scans 041_w0 slice 59 and 042_w0 slice 51 (Dice 0.00).** These slices lie below the bladder, close to the apex of the prostate. In the ground truth, the prostate appears as a small region directly in front of the rectum. The 3D model segmented the rectum correctly but failed to detect the prostate at all. The most likely explanation is the downsampling required to fit the volumes into memory. At a resolution of 128×128×64, the apex is only a few voxels wide, so very little information about it survives. Once this detail is lost, upsampling the prediction back to full resolution cannot recover it.

**Case 4 - 3D, scans 040_w5 slice 60 and 040_w2 slice 48 (Dice 0.48, 0.55).** In these cases the model located the prostate correctly but predicted a region noticeably smaller than the ground truth. This appears to have the same underlying cause as Case 3: halving the resolution in each dimension tends to smooth out the boundaries of small structures, which leads to systematic under-estimation of their size.

**Why it matters:** Under-segmentation at the base or apex is not a minor technical issue. In radiotherapy planning, it could result in part of the prostate edge receiving insufficient radiation dose. The results show that the 3D model’s improvement on boundary slices (Dice rising from 0.71 to 0.78) comes largely from correcting the 2D model’s failures at the base. However, the downsampling it depends on introduces a new weakness at the apex. In other words, 3D context helps, but only if enough spatial resolution is preserved.

**Fixes to try:** Two changes could address these failures. First, the 3D model could be trained at full resolution using patch-based sampling (for example, 128-voxel crops taken from the original 256×256×128 volumes), which would retain fine detail at the apex. Second, a boundary-weighted loss function could be introduced so that missed edge pixels are penalised more heavily during training.

**Caveat:** These findings should be interpreted with some caution. Slice-level Dice is highly sensitive when the ground truth contains only a few pixels, since a difference of just a handful of pixels can move the score from 0 to 1. It is also possible that some of these small fragments reflect partial-volume effects in the manual annotations rather than genuine prostate tissue.
